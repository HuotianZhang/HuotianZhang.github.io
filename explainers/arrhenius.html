Does heat help charges escape? Thermally activated charge separation

In the best organic solar cells, the energy offset that splits excitons has become very small. Some studies found that charge separation then needs thermal energy; others found it needs none. This study measures 14 donor–acceptor blends with one consistent definition of the offset. Below an offset of about 0.2 eV, charge separation becomes thermally activated and its efficiency drops. Between that and the high-offset regime lies a "mid-offset" range, where heat makes up for the small offset and neither current nor voltage is lost.

N. Jain, R. Jasiūnas, X. Li, H. Zhang, J. Fu, R. Zhang, G. Li, M. Fahlman, V. Gulbinas, F. Gao*. The Role of Thermally Activated Charge Separation in Organic Solar Cells. Adv. Energy Mater. 2024, 2403675. doi:10.1002/aenm.202403675 (* corresponding author)

1 / 9 · THE DEBATE

Barrier or no barrier?

An exciton that reaches the donor–acceptor interface first forms a charge-transfer (CT) state: an electron on the acceptor and a hole on the donor, still bound by their Coulomb attraction. In fullerene cells, an empirical rule said the CT state had to lie about 0.3 eV below the lowest excited state for the pair to split efficiently. Non-fullerene acceptors (NFAs) broke this rule, and efficiencies passed 19%.

How the pairs separate with so little excess energy was debated. One low-offset NFA blend showed thermally activated CT states. Another, PM6:Y6, showed barrierless, temperature-independent generation, explained by the large quadrupole moment of the acceptor. Most studies looked at a single blend, and "low offset" was defined differently from paper to paper.

2 / 9 · ONE RULER

Measuring the offset in the working device

Offsets from UPS or cyclic voltammetry vary a lot between groups, and they are measured on the neat materials. They miss the band bending from the acceptor's quadrupole moment and the vacuum-level shift from interface dipoles.

The authors use the blend itself. The offset is Eg − ECT0K. Eg comes from the probability distribution of the EQE spectrum (the EgPV definition). ECT0K comes from extrapolating VOC versus temperature to 0 K, where qVOC and the CT energy coincide. The uncertainty is ±0.01 eV. This offset follows the UPS offset monotonically and agrees between labs.

3 / 9 · 14 BLENDS

Below about 0.2 eV, the efficiency falls

For each blend the charge separation efficiency (CSE) is estimated as JSC/JSQ: the measured short-circuit current divided by the current a cell with 100% EQE above the gap would give. The authors call this a rough estimate, but good enough to see the trend.

Table 1 covers offsets from 0.03 to 0.63 eV. Above a transition range near 0.2 eV, CSE is roughly constant. Below it, CSE drops steeply: from 76% for PM6:Y6 (0.32 eV) to 3% for PM6:IEICO (0.03 eV). Literature data added in Fig. 1 follow the same trend.

4 / 9 · A CONTROLLED SERIES

Same acceptor, three donors

Could morphology or disorder cause the drop instead? To check, the authors compare three blends of the acceptor Y1 with three closely related donors: PM5 (PBDB-T), PM6 (PBDB-T-2F) and PM7 (PBDB-T-2Cl). The offset goes 0.23 → 0.17 → 0.11 eV, and the CSE 59 → 44 → 22%.

GIWAXS and GISAXS show similar crystallinity, pure-domain and mixed-domain sizes in all three. The Urbach energies are all 22–24 meV, and the donor HOMO disorder is similar. So neither morphology nor CT-state disorder explains the difference. The offset is the variable that changes.

5 / 9 · ULTRAFAST VIEW

Where the charges are lost

Transient absorption with 800 nm pulses excites only Y1, since the donors do not absorb there. In PM5:Y1 the acceptor bleach fades over 1–100 ps while the donor bleach grows: holes move from Y1 to the donor and stay separated. In PM6:Y1 and PM7:Y1, both bleaches already decay on a tens-of-ps time scale. This is geminate recombination: with a small offset, holes can transfer back to the acceptor and re-form its excited state, which then decays quickly.

The authors take the total bleach area (donor plus acceptor) at 100 ps relative to the area just after excitation. This gives about 50%, 29% and 25% for PM5:Y1, PM6:Y1 and PM7:Y1, close to the JSC/JSQ values.

6 / 9 · WARM IT UP

Current that grows with temperature

Next the cells are measured from 200 to 305 K under weak light (0.26 mW cm−2, about 10−3 sun), so that bimolecular recombination is negligible. Exciton dissociation at the interface hardly depends on temperature or offset here, so the change in current tracks the separation of CT states into free charges.

For high-offset PTO2:IT-4F (0.35 eV), the current stays the same: separation is already efficient at 200 K. For PM7:IEICO-4F (0.11 eV), the current at 305 K is twice that at 200 K. The pairs need thermal energy to get over the Coulomb barrier. An Arrhenius fit of the current gives an activation energy Ea for each blend.

Curves computed here as J(T)/J(200 K) = exp[−Ea/kB(1/T − 1/200 K)] with the Table 1 Ea; they agree with the data of Fig. 3a within reading accuracy (×2.0 for PM7:IEICO-4F at 305 K).

7 / 9 · MIRROR IMAGE

Activation energy and efficiency move in opposite directions

Plotted against offset, Ea and CSE are mirror images. In high-offset blends, Ea is below the thermal energy at room temperature (about 26 meV) and CSE is high. PTO2:IT-4F has only 1.9 meV.

In low-offset blends Ea rises above 26 meV: 35–39 meV for PM7:Y1, PM6:IEICO-4F and PM7:IEICO-4F, and 44 meV for PM6:IEICO. These pairs face a real Coulomb barrier and many fail to separate at room temperature. The CSE that remains in these blends is partly thanks to heat.

8 / 9 · THREE REGIMES

Shrinking the offset helps only up to a point

High offset: excess energy separates the charges, and CSE stays high as the offset shrinks while VOC rises (with the same low-gap material). PM6:Y6 (0.32 eV) → PM7:Y6 (0.22 eV): VOC goes from 0.83 to 0.87 V with no change in CSE.

Low offset: CSE falls sharply, but VOC no longer improves. The CT state and the local exciton are thermally populated together, so non-radiative recombination is dominated by the exciton of the low-gap material, and VOC is set by its luminescence yield. PM6:Y1 (0.17 eV) → PM7:Y1 (0.11 eV): CSE halves, VOC stays the same.

Mid offset: a narrow range in between, where excess energy and thermal energy together keep CSE high while VOC is already at its maximum.

9 / 9 · THE SWEET SPOT

About 15 meV is enough

How large may Ea be? Starting from the measured temperature dependence of PTO2:IT-4F (Ea = 1.9 meV), the authors model the power output versus temperature for larger activation energies. The higher Ea, the higher the temperature at which output peaks. Measured cells follow the same trend (Fig. 4b). An Ea close to 15 meV is still enough for optimal CSE at room temperature; much higher values will likely hurt.

PM1:BTP-eC9, one of the best binary blends (PCE over 19%), fits this picture: offset ≈ 0.2 eV, voltage loss 0.5 V, Ea ≈ 8 meV and CSE 81%. The authors' advice: don't aim for zero offset. Aim for the mid-offset range and tune the activation energy, for example through hybridisation, interface dipoles or quadrupole moments. These results are for weak light. At 1 sun, bimolecular recombination at low temperature changes the picture.

Try it

Heat a bound pair

A toy, not a simulation from the paper. A CT pair either escapes over a barrier Eb or recombines. The escape rate is k0·exp(−Eb/kBT); recombination has a fixed rate kr. The toy separation yield is then 1 / (1 + (kr/k0)·exp(Eb/kBT)). Eb and kr/k0 exist only in this toy. The lab then does what the paper does with real currents: it fits an Arrhenius law between 200 and 305 K and reports the apparent activation energy.

–toy yield at 295 K
–current ratio 305 K / 200 K
–apparent Ea (fit 200–305 K)

Try this: a small barrier gives a flat current, as for PTO2:IT-4F. Raise Eb until the current doubles between 200 and 305 K, as for PM7:IEICO-4F. The apparent Ea then comes out near 35 meV, and well below Eb: once the yield nears 100%, the current stops growing and the fit sees a smaller slope. Lowering kr/k0 (slower recombination) has a similar effect.
