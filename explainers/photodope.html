Small counterions make light-driven doping work better

Photocatalytic doping uses a dye that turns into a strong oxidant only when it absorbs light. It pulls electrons out of a polymer film and hands them on to oxygen from the air. A simple salt provides the anions that sit next to the new positive charges. This study shows that the salt does more than that. Small anions keep the dye molecules from stacking in solution, so more of them stay active. As a result, PBTTT reaches 970 S cm⁻¹ with the smallest anion, compared with 187 S cm⁻¹ with the largest.

T. Liu, Z. Liu, I. Sahalianov, Q. He, S. Y. Jeong, H. Zhang, Q. Li, C.-Y. Yang, J. Ji, L. Bynens, W. Maes, F. Gao, H. Y. Woo, M. Heeney, G. Baryshnikov, M. Fahlman & S. Fabiano*. Counterion-controlled photocatalytic doping of organic semiconductors. Advanced Materials 38, e72947 (2026). doi:10.1002/adma.72947 (* corresponding author · open access, CC BY 4.0)

1 / 9 · THE CYCLE

A dye that oxidizes only in the light

Doping an organic semiconductor means adding or removing electrons. Normally that takes a strong molecular dopant, and strong dopants tend to be reactive and unstable. Photocatalytic doping avoids them. A thin polymer film is dipped into an acetonitrile solution of a photocatalyst and lit with blue light (455 nm, 50 mW cm⁻²).

The photocatalyst here is 10-methylacridinium (Acr-Me+). It is harmless in the dark. Its excited state, Acr-Me+*, takes an electron from the polymer PBTTT, which leaves a positive polaron on the chain. Oxygen from the air then takes the electron back, so the catalyst returns to its starting state. Only the oxygen and the salt are used up. The Li+ salt supplies the anions that move into the film to balance the polarons' charge.

Schematic, after Fig. 1a. The film is ~30 nm thick; the solution holds 10 mM Acr-Me⁺ and 100 mM Li⁺ salt (Methods).

2 / 9 · NO SALT, NO DOPING

The counterion is not optional

Without any added salt the doped PBTTT film reaches only 1.31 ± 0.23 S cm⁻¹. With LiTFSI, the salt used in the group's earlier work, it reaches 659 ± 188 S cm⁻¹, more than two orders of magnitude higher.

A polaron left on its own is unstable. It needs a negative ion close by, and the only anions that can enter the film come from the salt. Up to now the salt was treated as passive, a supply of counterions that does not change how fast the electron transfer runs.

3 / 9 · SIZE MATTERS

The smaller the anion, the higher the conductivity

The authors tested four lithium salts of the same family of imide anions. Their side groups grow from F (FSI−) through CF3 (TFSI−) and C2F5 (PFSI−) to C4F9 (NFSI−). Conductivity falls as the anion grows: 970 ± 107 S cm⁻¹ with FSI−, 659 ± 188 with TFSI− and 187 ± 40 with NFSI−.

The trend is the same in thin and thick films, so it is not about how deep ions can diffuse. It also holds for other acridinium catalysts and for the glycolated polymer PgBTTT, which reaches 2005 ± 160 S cm⁻¹ with FSI−. Other doping methods, such as ion-exchange doping, usually work best with large counterions. That opposite trend hints at a different mechanism.

4 / 9 · WATCHING IT HAPPEN

The neutral band fades, the polaron band grows

Absorption spectra track the doping directly. Neutral PBTTT absorbs at 550 nm. Polarons absorb in the infrared, monitored here at 1600 nm. With FSI− the 550 nm peak bleaches fast and the infrared band rises strongly within the first 5 minutes of light. After 10 minutes the neutral peak is almost gone, so the film is fully doped.

With NFSI− the bleaching is much slower, and a residual neutral peak is still there after 15 minutes. Conductivity rises fastest with FSI− over the same first 5 minutes.

Schematic, after Fig. 2a,b: band shapes and time courses are drawn, not digitised.

5 / 9 · COUNTING CHARGES

Three methods give the same order

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy separates the sulfur signal of neutral and oxidized thiophene units. The oxidized fraction, used as the doping level, is 41%, 37%, 33% and 16% for FSI−, TFSI−, PFSI− and NFSI−. The counterions' own O, N and F signals follow the same order, so fewer anions enter the film as they grow.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), which detects the unpaired spins of polarons, weakens from FSI− to NFSI−. Pristine PBTTT shows no signal. The Seebeck coefficient, which falls as carrier density rises, is lowest with FSI− (23.44 ± 0.80 µV K⁻¹) and highest with NFSI− (38.62 ± 0.45 µV K⁻¹).

Values from the text (Fig. 2c–e, Fig. S11). Changing the assumed XPS peak shift by ±0.1 eV changes the doping levels by only ~3–4%.

6 / 9 · INSIDE THE FILM

Anions settle among the side chains

X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) shows that PBTTT stays edge-on after doping, with its backbones standing upright on the substrate. Doping widens the lamellar spacing, the distance between backbones set apart by the side chains, and slightly shrinks the π–π stacking distance. Both changes fit counterions sitting mainly among the side chains.

From FSI− to NFSI− the π–π distance grows from 3.57 to 3.67 Å, and the lamellar spacing falls from 27.92 to 26.62 Å. These changes track the lower doping level. Fewer anions inside means less swelling and looser π-stacking.

7 / 9 · THE CATALYST STACKS

Large anions leave the dye aggregated

The key evidence comes from the solution. Dilute Acr-Me+ (0.1 mM) absorbs at 358 and 416 nm, and the salts leave these peaks alone. A broad band between 500 and 700 nm, however, grows as the anion gets larger. It is stronger at 10 mM, the concentration used for doping. This band is charge-transfer absorption between stacked acridinium ions, a sign of aggregates. The 10 mM solution looks bright yellow with FSI− and dark green with NFSI−. The salts themselves are transparent and redox-inert.

Time-resolved fluorescence, measured in Huotian's part of the work, shows that the excited catalyst lives longer with small anions: 31.28 ns (FSI−), 26.99 ns (TFSI−), 24.08 ns (PFSI−) and 21.72 ns (NFSI−). A longer-lived excited state has more time to reach the polymer and take an electron.

Peak positions and lifetimes from the text (Fig. 3a–c, Fig. S21). Spectra and vial colours are schematic, after Fig. 3b,c. The PL experiments were done by H. Zhang and F. Gao (author contributions).

8 / 9 · WHY SMALL WINS

FSI⁻ wedges the stacks apart

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reproduce the 450–700 nm band for stacks of two to four Acr-Me+ ions. The bulkier anions (TFSI−, PFSI−, NFSI−) stabilise the stacks through van der Waals contacts. Only the small FSI− pushes into or next to a dimer, increases the spacing between the ions and weakens the aggregate band. The more FSI− ions, the larger the distortion.

Breaking up the stacks also changes the electron affinity (EA) of the catalyst. Measured EA levels are −4.35 eV for pristine (aggregated) Acr-Me+, −4.37 eV with NFSI− and −4.51 eV with FSI−, close to the monomer. DFT gives a ~0.1 eV increase with FSI−. The shift is modest, but the group earlier saw several-fold conductivity gains from 0.1–0.2 eV EA differences between catalysts. Together with more free catalyst molecules, it can explain the better doping.

9 / 9 · THE CONTROL

Take the salt away, and the difference goes too

If the anion acted only inside the film, its size should matter at any concentration. The test: use only 0.1 equivalents of LiFSI or LiNFSI instead of 10. At this low dose both solutions show a similar 500–700 nm aggregate band, and the doped films reach similar conductivities.

So the counterion acts already in the solution, where electrons are transferred, by controlling how many catalyst molecules are free and active. Counterion size is a new design handle for photocatalytic doping. The authors note that the microscopic details may differ for other acridinium catalysts.

Try it

Aggregates versus active catalyst

A toy, not a simulation from the paper. A fraction f of the catalyst is free (monomeric); stacked molecules are taken as inactive. A free excited catalyst either takes an electron from the polymer, with a toy rate kq = 0.1 ns⁻¹, or relaxes with its lifetime τ, so the share of excitations that dope is kqτ/(1 + kqτ). The film's oxidized fraction p then rises as pmax(1 − e−rt). The rate r is proportional to f times that share, and the toy saturation pmax is 0.5. The canvas shows the resulting toy absorption spectrum: neutral band ∝ 1 − p/pmax, polaron band ∝ p. Presets set τ to the measured fluorescence lifetimes; f is a guess in both.

Try this: press “FSI⁻-like” (f = 0.8, τ = 31.28 ns) and “NFSI⁻-like” (f = 0.3, τ = 21.72 ns) at 5 min. The lifetime alone changes the doping share only a little. Most of the gap in the toy comes from f, the free catalyst. The paper does not measure f; it argues from the aggregate band, the lifetimes and the EA shift together. In the toy, every curve eventually saturates at the same pmax. In the paper the NFSI⁻ film stays less doped (16% vs 41% by XPS), so the real process is not only slower.
