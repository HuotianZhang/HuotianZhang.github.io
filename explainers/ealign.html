Explainer · Nature Communications 2022 · Paper

A step in the vacuum level at the donor/acceptor interface

Energy diagrams of organic solar cells are usually drawn from neat-film energy levels, as if the vacuum level stayed flat across the donor/acceptor interface. Drawn that way, the four high-performing pairs studied here show HOMO offsets of 0.68–0.91 eV. This study builds donor/acceptor bilayers one molecular layer at a time and measures the energy levels layer by layer. The vacuum level jumps by up to 0.7 eV within one or two layers of the interface, and the energy levels do not bend. The corrected diagrams have near-zero offsets and larger interfacial gaps, in line with the charge-transfer energies and voltages measured in devices.

X. Li*, Q. Zhang, J. Yu, Y. Xu, R. Zhang, C. Wang, H. Zhang, S. Fabiano, X. Liu, J. Hou, F. Gao & M. Fahlman*. Mapping the energy level alignment at donor/acceptor interfaces in non-fullerene organic solar cells. Nature Communications 13, 2046 (2022). doi:10.1038/s41467-022-29702-w (* corresponding authors)

1 / 9 · A CONTRADICTION

Neat-film levels predict a gap that is too small

Charges are made, and lost, at the interface between donor and acceptor. So the energy level alignment there sets the driving force for charge separation and the energy of the charge-transfer (CT) state. Fullerene cells usually needed an offset of more than 0.3 eV. Many non-fullerene (NFA) cells work with offsets near zero, but those offsets were mostly estimated from optics or cyclic voltammetry. Other studies, which measured HOMO levels by photoelectron spectroscopy, concluded that a HOMO offset of about 0.5 eV is still needed.

The trouble shows when you draw the usual diagram. For PM6 and IT4F, the neat-film levels used in this paper give ΔHOMO = 0.73 eV and a photovoltaic gap Γ (donor HOMO to acceptor LUMO) of only 0.96 eV. Yet the CT state measured in the PM6:IT4F device sits at 1.46 eV. A CT state, with the electron on the acceptor and the hole on the donor, would be expected to lie at or below that gap, not 0.5 eV above it. The flat-vacuum-level assumption behind the diagram had never been tested for NFA interfaces.

2 / 9 · TWO WAYS OUT

Bend the levels, or shift the vacuum level

The interfacial gap could be larger than the neat-film diagram says in two ways. In the energy level bending picture, used in the earlier photoelectron study, quadrupole fields at the interface bend the HOMO and LUMO over some distance. The vacuum level stays put.

In the vacuum level shift picture, a dipole layer sits right at the interface. It makes the vacuum level jump by Δ. All levels on one side move rigidly with it, and nothing bends. Both pictures can raise the interfacial gap from Γ to Γ′. They predict different depth profiles, though. Bending changes the HOMO position layer by layer near the interface; a dipole step changes the work function abruptly while the HOMO, measured from the local vacuum level, stays the same.

3 / 9 · MEASURING IT

Photoelectrons read the top layer

Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) gives two numbers for a film on a grounded substrate, both referenced to the Fermi level. The secondary-electron cut-off gives the work function (WF), which is where the local vacuum level sits. The valence-band edge, added to the WF, gives the ionization potential (IP), the HOMO energy below vacuum.

First test: spin-coat a donor (PBDB-T or PM6) on gold, then an acceptor (ITIC or IT4F) on top from an orthogonal solvent. Adding the acceptor raised the WF by 0.60–0.71 eV in all four pairs. The IPs equalled those of the neat acceptors (ITIC 5.74, IT4F 5.79 ± 0.1 eV). But UPS only sees the topmost molecular layer. It cannot tell where in the stack the WF changed, or what the IP is right at the interface.

4 / 9 · ONE LAYER AT A TIME

Building the interface monolayer by monolayer

These large molecules cannot be evaporated, and spin-coated layers mix. So the authors used the Langmuir–Schäfer (LS) technique. A dilute solution spreads on water; two barriers compress the floating film into a closed monolayer (at 25 mN m⁻¹ for polymers, 30 mN m⁻¹ for small molecules); a substrate touches it horizontally and picks it up. Repeating this gives stacks with sharp, unmixed interfaces.

Surface pressure, AFM and X-ray absorption (NEXAFS) showed that all LS films are edge-on (IT4F a little less ordered). UPS was measured after 1, 2, 3, 6 and 10 monolayers of the bottom material, then after 1, 2, 3, 6 and 10 monolayers of the top one. Two stacking orders were built: donor on gold with acceptor on top, and acceptor on AlOx with donor on top.

5 / 9 · THE DEPTH PROFILE

The work function jumps; the IP does not drift

At the substrate the films behave as established models predict, with any WF change complete after one or two monolayers. At the donor/acceptor interface the WF jumps again, within 1–2 monolayers. It goes up when the acceptor is on top and down when the donor is on top. After that it stays flat.

The IP is already at its bulk value in the very first monolayer next to the interface, and stays there up to 10 monolayers. That holds for every donor and every acceptor, in both stacking orders. So the HOMO does not bend, and by inference the LUMO does not either. What is left is an abrupt vacuum level shift: the second picture.

Measured data from the paper's Source Data for Fig. 2 (all four pairs; one highlighted at a time). The 0 nm point is the bare substrate's WF.

6 / 9 · ALWAYS THE SAME SIGN

A dipole that points from acceptor to donor

Four ways of making the bilayer, each giving different molecular orientations, all gave the same sign. Acceptor on top of donor: the WF rises (ΔWF from +0.32 to +0.71 eV). Donor on top of acceptor: it falls (−0.10 to −0.36 eV). So every interface carries a dipole that points from the acceptor side to the donor side, with its positive end on the donor.

Its size depends on how the bilayer was made. Spin-coated/spin-coated films, which contain face-on/face-on contacts as in real blends, give the largest shifts. Donor-bottom films show larger steps than acceptor-bottom ones; the authors tentatively relate this to the small acceptor molecules finding their best position at the interface more easily than the bulky polymers. Bilayers on ITO/PEDOT:PSS and ITO/ZnO gave similar values.

7 / 9 · THE CORRECTED DIAGRAM

Offsets shrink, the interfacial gap grows

Using the spin-coated shifts (closest to device morphology), the authors redrew all four diagrams. Before contact, every pair shows ΔHOMO and ΔLUMO above 0.6 eV and a Γ of only 0.78–1.21 eV. After the vacuum level shift, the acceptor levels move up rigidly by Δ.

For PM6/ITIC, ΔHOMO drops from 0.68 to 0.08 eV and Γ grows from 1.21 to 1.81 eV. For PM6/IT4F, ΔHOMO becomes 0.02 eV. For the two PBDB-T pairs, 0.26 eV is left. The HOMO offset that the earlier study inferred from neat films is largely cancelled by the dipole at the interface. The authors see this as largely confirming the earlier photoelectron study, with one important difference: there is no level bending, but a dipole step that turns a large neat-film ΔHOMO into a near-zero one at the interface.

8 / 9 · CHECKED AGAINST DEVICES

Γ′, then ECT, then qVOC

To test the corrected diagrams, the four blends were made into solar cells (inverted, ITO/ZnO/blend/MoO3/Ag). The CT energy of each device was obtained by fitting the reduced EQEPV and electroluminescence spectra and taking their crossing point. In the author list, this CT-energy determination is Huotian Zhang's contribution.

Without the correction, Γ (0.78–1.21 eV) lies below ECT (1.31–1.59 eV) for every blend. With it, Γ′ (1.43–1.81 eV) lies 0.12–0.22 eV above ECT, and qVOC another 0.59–0.64 eV below that. Every blend shows the same cascade, Γ′ > ECT > qVOC. The blend with the largest Γ′ (PM6:ITIC, 1.81 eV) has the highest VOC (1.00 V); the one with the smallest (PBDB-T:IT4F, 1.43 eV) has the lowest (0.69 V). A large photovoltaic gap with a near-zero HOMO offset fits efficient charge generation together with small voltage loss.

9 / 9 · WHERE DOES THE DIPOLE COME FROM?

Push–pull molecules and a large neat-film offset

The usual model for organic interfaces (integer charge transfer, ICT) predicts much smaller steps. The authors subtracted the ICT estimate from the measured shift to get an "enhanced" vacuum level shift, for 25 donor/acceptor combinations including Y6, Y11, IEICO, fullerenes, D18, TQ1, P3HT and PFO. Enhanced shifts above 0.1 eV appear only when the neat-film HOMO offset exceeds 0.3 eV, and mainly when both partners are push–pull (alternating donor–acceptor) molecules. P3HT:ITIC is the one exception without a push–pull donor. The C60 pairs show essentially none.

The tentative explanation is electrostatic. Push–pull molecules have strongly varying surface potentials: donors mostly negative, acceptors mostly positive. Calculations on donor and acceptor units stacked face to face (3.5 Å apart) give dipoles of 0.73–0.93 Debye pointing from the A unit to the D unit. A dense layer of such dipoles shifts the vacuum level by ΔVL = q·μ₀/(ε₀εrA). With A = 20 Å² and εr = 3 this gives 0.45–0.58 eV, against 0.60–0.71 eV measured.

A calculator, not a simulation from the paper.
