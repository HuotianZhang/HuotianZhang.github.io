Which gap? Measuring voltage loss in organic solar cells

The voltage loss of a solar cell is its optical gap minus its open-circuit voltage. Organic semiconductors have no sharp band gap, though, and groups define the gap in different ways. This report compares four definitions and shows how much the answer depends on the choice. With a consistent reference, the best organic cells of 2018 still lost not much less than 0.6 V.

Y. Wang, D. Qian, Y. Cui, H. Zhang, J. Hou, K. Vandewal, T. Kirchartz*, F. Gao*. Optical Gaps of Organic Solar Cells as a Reference for Comparing Voltage Losses. Adv. Energy Mater. 8, 1801352 (2018). doi:10.1002/aenm.201801352 (* corresponding authors)

1 / 9 · THE QUESTION

One cell, three voltage losses

A common figure of merit for a solar cell is its voltage loss, ΔVOC = Eg/q − VOC. When this report was written, many papers claimed "low" losses between about 0.47 and 0.6 V. But each paper took the optical gap Eg from the spectra in its own way, so the numbers could not be compared directly.

Take the PBDB-T:IEICO-4Cl cell from the report's Table 3, with VOC = 0.74 V. Read its gap from the onset of the quantum-efficiency (EQE) spectrum and the loss is 0.51 V. Use the two other EQE-based definitions and you get 0.57 V or 0.62 V. The device is the same; only the definition changes.

2 / 9 · STEP VERSUS SMEAR

Organic semiconductors have no sharp edge

In the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit the absorptance is a step: 1 above the band gap, 0 below it. The gap is then simply the energy of the step. A cell that absorbs must also emit, and its emission is the black-body spectrum multiplied by that step, so it peaks right at the gap.

A real organic blend is disordered and has no clean gap in its density of states. Its absorptance rises gradually. Below the gap there are weak features from charge-transfer (CT) states at the donor–acceptor interface, and because the black-body factor favours low energies, these weak CT features often dominate the emission. Any "gap" for such a material is a practical definition, much like the E04 gap used for amorphous silicon (the energy where the absorption coefficient reaches 104 cm−1).

3 / 9 · THREE LOSSES

Where the definition enters

The report splits the voltage loss into three parts (its Eq. 1):

ΔVOC = (Eg/q − VOCSQ) + (VOCSQ − VOCrad) + (VOCrad − VOC) = ΔV1 + ΔV2 + ΔV3

VOCSQ is the SQ voltage for a step at Eg, so it depends on the chosen gap. VOCrad is the voltage the cell would reach if radiative recombination were the only loss. It is calculated from the measured EQE spectrum and needs no gap at all.

So the choice of Eg moves ΔV1 and ΔV2. The non-radiative loss ΔV3 is the same whatever definition you use.

4 / 9 · WHAT MOVES

ΔV1 barely moves, so ΔV2 takes the change

ΔV1 is the unavoidable radiative loss of an ideal SQ cell, about 0.2–0.3 V depending on the gap. Plotted against Eg, the curves of Eg/q and VOCSQ rise almost in parallel, so ΔV1 changes very little when the definition changes. The difference almost all goes into ΔV2.

ΔV2 comes from the absorption edge not being a step. It has a small part from the lower short-circuit current, ΔV2SC, and a larger part, ΔV20, because emission sits below the gap. In organic cells ΔV20 is unusually large because of the red-shifted CT states. In newer blends with almost no energy offset, the CT absorption and emission disappear and ΔV20 shrinks.

5 / 9 · DEFINITION 1

The onset: popular, but arbitrary

The most common choice is to fit a straight line to the absorption edge and take its intersection with the energy axis. The result depends on which part of the edge you fit, light scattering in spin-coated films blurs the tail, and there is no physical basis for the construction. Blending can also shift the edge: a PIPCP:PC61BM film absorbs about 70 meV further to the red than neat PIPCP.

The onset also gives the smallest gap and hence the smallest losses. For PBDB-T:IEICO-4Cl (Table 1), a neat-film onset of 1.25 eV gives VOCSQ ≈ 0.99 V, but the cell's VOCrad is ≈ 1.02 V. That makes ΔV2 negative (−0.03 V): the "ideal" SQ cell would be worse than the real cell's radiative limit. For a voltage-loss analysis, the report concludes, this gap is not helpful at all.

Table 1 values: Eg 1.25, VOCSQ 0.99, VOCrad 1.02 V; ΔV1 0.25, ΔV2 −0.03, ΔV3 0.29, ΔVOC 0.51 V. Left panel schematic, after Fig. 2a.

6 / 9 · DEFINITION 2

Where absorption meets emission

A physically motivated choice is the 0–0 energy E0−0, the gap between the relaxed ground state and the relaxed lowest excited singlet. Absorption peaks above it and emission below it, each shifted by a relaxation energy:

Emax,abs = E0−0 + λabs    Emax,fl = E0−0 − λfl

When the two spectra are roughly mirror images where they overlap, as for most organic semiconductors, E0−0 is where the normalised absorptance and photoluminescence cross. The method is reproducible and has a clear meaning. Its drawbacks: not every group reports emission spectra, and blending can shift them. For IEICO-4Cl in PTB7-Th the blend absorption edge moves by ≈ 20 meV and the PL by ≈ 10 meV. Table 2 lists neat-film values, for example 1.67 eV for PTB7-Th and 1.33 eV for IEICO-4Cl.

7 / 9 · DEFINITIONS 3 AND 4

Reading the gap from the solar cell itself

The EQE spectrum of the finished device is easy to measure. Unlike film spectra, it already includes any changes the blend morphology causes. (It does depend slightly on device optics such as layer thickness.) Two definitions use it:

Egedge (Vandewal and co-workers): extrapolate the EQE edge and see where it meets the horizontal tangent at the EQE peak. A sharper edge puts it closer to the onset.

EgPV (Rau and co-workers): treat the measured EQE as a mix of SQ steps with different gaps. The share of each step is P(E) = dEQE/dE, and EgPV is the mean of P(E) between the two energies a and b where P falls to half its maximum. The ½ is arbitrary, but small changes to it hardly matter unless the data are noisy.

8 / 9 · 32 CELLS

No trade-off between EQE and voltage loss

The authors collected 32 published cells, with fullerene and non-fullerene acceptors, and recalculated each one's loss with three EQE-based gaps (Table 3). The same cell can look very different: TQ1:PC71BM loses 0.81 V by the onset, 0.94 V by EgPV and 1.08 V by Egedge.

A 2015 analysis of mostly fullerene cells had suggested that a high peak EQE needs a large voltage loss, a large energy offset to split the excitons. In these 32 cells there is no clear trend with any of the three definitions. Peak EQE can stay high even as ΔVOC approaches about 0.6 V.

Every point is a Table 3 row (Fig. 6a–c). Blue: fullerene acceptors; red: non-fullerene acceptors and the 6T/rubrene/SubNc/SubPc stack.

9 / 9 · OUTLOOK

Not much below 0.6 V, and about 18%

The onset gap marks where absorption is zero. Efficiency predictions assume a constant EQE above the gap, so the onset is no use for them. With EgPV or Egedge, no organic cell up to that point lost much less than 0.6 V.

Using a published prediction with EQE fixed at 85% and a fill factor of 75%, these smallest losses correspond to a realistic single-junction efficiency limit of about 17–18%. To reach the efficiencies of inorganic and perovskite cells, the voltage loss itself had to come down. The authors' recommendation: use EgPV or Egedge, which come straight from the EQE and link the SQ limit to real devices.

Smallest losses per definition from Table 3: onset 0.48 V, EgPV 0.53 V, Egedge 0.59 V. ≈ 17–18% from Fig. 6d.
