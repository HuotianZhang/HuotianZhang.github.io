Explainer · Nature Energy 2023

Why a third component raises, pins or lowers the voltage

The best organic solar cells mix a guest material into a donor–acceptor blend. Sometimes this raises the open-circuit voltage, sometimes it does nothing, sometimes it lowers it. This paper splits the voltage into a radiative part and a non-radiative loss. It shows that the loss is set by which excited states are thermally populated, and it ends with two design rules for the guest.

Y. Wang, J. Yu, R. Zhang, J. Yuan, S. Hultmark, C. E. Johnson, N. P. Gallop, B. Siegmund, D. Qian, H. Zhang, Y. Zou, M. Kemerink, A. A. Bakulin, C. Müller, K. Vandewal, X.-K. Chen, F. Gao. Origins of the open-circuit voltage in ternary organic solar cells and design rules for minimized voltage losses. Nature Energy (2023). doi:10.1038/s41560-023-01309-5

1 / 9 · THE PUZZLE

Up, flat or down

A ternary organic solar cell (TOSC) is a host binary blend (one donor, one acceptor) plus a guest component. Record efficiencies come from such cells. At first the guest was added to absorb more light, but it can also change the open-circuit voltage VOC, and nobody agreed on why.

The authors built six dual-acceptor systems. In each one a fullerene (PC71BM or Bis-PC62BM) gradually replaces the non-fullerene acceptor: PM6:Y6, PBDB-T:Y1, PM6:IT-M (twice, with two fullerenes), PM6:ITCC and PBDB-T:IEICO-4F. Depending on the system, VOC rises, stays pinned or falls as the fullerene content goes up.

2 / 9 · OLD MODELS

Alloy or parallel?

Two models were in use. The alloy model assumes the two acceptors mix well and form one phase with an averaged charge-transfer (CT) state. The parallel model assumes poor mixing, so the cell behaves like two binary cells side by side.

Each works in some systems and fails in others. The alloy model cannot explain tunable VOC in some cells with two similar fullerenes. Some "parallel-like" cells have VOC pinned to the lower binary value instead of a linear mix. Most of all, both tie VOC only to the CT-state energy ECT. Neither considers how the guest changes recombination, even though the non-radiative loss is a major part of the voltage loss.

3 / 9 · TWO PARTS

Radiative limit minus non-radiative loss

Detailed balance (assuming near thermal equilibrium) splits the voltage into two measurable pieces:

VOC = VOCrad − ΔVnr

VOCrad = (kT/q) ln(JSC/J0,rad) comes from the photovoltaic quantum efficiency EQEPV. JSC weights it with the solar spectrum, which is nearly flat here. J0,rad weights it with 300 K black-body radiation, which grows exponentially towards low photon energy. So J0,rad is set by the weak absorption tail.

ΔVnr = −(kT/q) ln(EQEEL)

EQEEL is the electroluminescence efficiency of the cell run as an LED. Computed this way, VOC follows the measured trends in all six systems.

4 / 9 · TWO CASES

Does the guest change the host?

Treat the guest as forming its own binary blend with the donor (the "guest binary"). Then compare the ternary EQEPV with the composition-weighted sum of the two binary spectra.

Case-1 (five systems): the two match. The ternary behaves like a linear combination of two binaries, and the morphology hardly changes. For PM6:Y6:PC71BM, calorimetry (DSC) and X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) look like those of PM6:Y6.

Case-2 (PBDB-T:IEICO-4F:PC71BM): the ternary absorption edge shifts further to the blue as fullerene is added. PC71BM suppresses aggregation of IEICO-4F: its melting endotherm at about 325 °C disappears and its diffraction peak broadens.

5 / 9 · RADIATIVE LIMIT

VOCrad barely moves

In Case-1, JSC and J0,rad of the ternary are weighted sums of the binary values. Write the guest's values as JSCG = a·JSCH and J0,radG = b·J0,radH. For guest fraction x:

VOCrad,T − VOCrad,H = (kT/q) ln[(1 − x + ax)/(1 − x + bx)]

In the measured systems a is 0.5–1.5 and b spans 10−5 to 101. With a = 1, a guest with smaller J0,rad (b < 1) gains at most about 20 mV at 50%. With b = 1 nothing changes. A guest with larger J0,rad (b > 1) pulls VOCrad down quickly. So in Case-1 the radiative limit leaves little room. In Case-2 the equation fails, because the host itself changes.

6 / 9 · NON-RADIATIVE LOSS

Three EQEEL trends

The host binaries emit at least ten times more efficiently than the guest binaries. In the host blends CT and local-exciton (LE) states lie only about 30–80 meV apart. They hybridise, and the bright LE state is thermally populated. In the fullerene guest blends the gap is about 150–340 meV, and the emission is pure, dim CT emission.

As the guest is added, EQEEL follows one of three trends. It stays pinned at the host value up to about 80% guest (PM6:Y6, PBDB-T:Y1, PM6:IT-M:Bis-PC62BM). It falls steadily (PM6:ITCC, PM6:IT-M:PC71BM). Or it rises up to about 80% and then drops (the Case-2 IEICO-4F system). In the electroluminescence spectra, guest CT emission shows up only in the systems where EQEEL falls.

7 / 9 · THERMAL POPULATION

Excitations sit where energy is lowest

Whether guest CT emission appears depends on where the guest CT state lies. In the "falling" systems ECT of the guest is below that of the host. In the "pinned" systems it is above. Near thermal equilibrium, excitations spread over all states according to the Boltzmann factor. A low-lying dim state collects them and becomes the main loss channel.

The authors extend their three-state model to four states: LEH 1.50 eV, CTH 1.45 eV, LEG 1.80 eV, and CTG moved from 1.60 to 1.30 eV. If ECT,G > ECT,H, the simulated EQEEL stays pinned to the host. If it is lower, EQEEL falls continuously with guest content. If the two are equal, EQEEL is roughly a linear mix of the two binaries. Thermal population alone reproduces all the Case-1 trends.

8 / 9 · CASE-2

Less aggregation, brighter host

The rise in the IEICO-4F system needs one more ingredient. When PC71BM breaks up IEICO-4F aggregates, aggregation-caused quenching (ACQ) is suppressed. The PLQY and the exciton lifetime of IEICO-4F rise with fullerene content (transient absorption shows the longer lifetime is not a fluence effect). In the model, a higher LEH PLQY lifts the flat part of the EQEEL curve, so EQEEL first rises and then falls, as measured.

Trap passivation could give a similar effect. Space-charge-limited-current measurements rule it out here. So in Case-2 both parts can improve: the blue-shifted edge raises VOCrad, and the brighter host lowers ΔVnr.

9 / 9 · DESIGN RULES

Two rules for the guest

Rule 1 (Case-1). Use a guest whose binary blend emits more efficiently than the host (EQEELG > EQEELH): a luminescent guest with a small CT–LE offset. Keep its CT energy close to the host's (ECT,G ≅ ECT,H). Too low, and absorption from those low states raises J0,rad (b > 1), so VOCrad falls by more than ΔVnr gains.

Rule 2 (Case-2). Use a guest that mixes well with the low-gap component of the host, so that it suppresses aggregation. Such a guest does not even need to be a semiconductor; an insulator could work, as long as it does not harm the electronic properties.

The two cases can overlap: one guest can meet both rules. The authors note that they do not exclude other mechanisms in other material combinations.

Try it

A four-state toy

This is a toy, not the paper's simulation. It uses the four level energies of Fig. 3g and the recombination rates and EQEEL factors from the Methods. Unlike the paper's model it has no electronic coupling, so there is no hybridisation. Excitations are spread over the four states by Boltzmann factors at 300 K, with host states weighted by (1 − x) and guest states by x. The radiative-limit change uses Eq. 5 with a = 1. The paper measures b; here it is guessed from the CT energies, b = exp[(ECT,H − ECT,G)/kT]. Read trends, not values.

–EQEEL (toy), host =
–change in ΔVnr
–change in VOCrad (Eq. 5, toy b)
–net change in VOC

Try this: start at the Fig. 3g values (1.60 eV, 0.20 eV) and the voltage barely moves. Drop ECT,G to 1.30 eV and the dim guest CT state soaks up the excitations. Then set ECT,G = 1.45 eV and shrink the offset below 0.05 eV: a bright guest at matched CT energy lowers ΔVnr with no radiative penalty. That is rule 1.
