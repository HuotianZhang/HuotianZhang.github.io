Two dark triplets, one bright singlet: triplet–triplet annihilation in organic solar cells

When free charges recombine in an organic solar cell, three times out of four they form a dark triplet exciton, and that costs voltage. Two triplets can fuse into one bright singlet, a trick that OLEDs already use. This study compares PM6:Y6 with PM6:Y11 and finds that fusion is the main way triplets decay in the more ordered Y11 blend at high excitation density. In principle it could win back tens of millivolts, but only if the triplets lived about a microsecond, much longer than in these acceptors.

1 / 9 · THE TRIPLET TAX

Three in four recombinations go dark

A solar cell at open circuit is also a light-emitting diode. The more of its recombination gives light, the higher its voltage: the non-radiative loss is ΔVnr = −kBT ln(EQEEL). The paper splits the electroluminescence efficiency into four factors (the first γ is a charge-balance factor, not the TTA rate used later):

EQEEL = γ · φPL · χ · ηout

χ is the fraction of recombination events that can give light at all. When a free electron and hole meet, their spins are random: 25% of pairs form singlets and 75% form triplets. A triplet exciton on the acceptor decays without light, by triplet–charge annihilation (TCA) or on its own, which adds up to 60 mV to ΔVnr.

In triplet–triplet annihilation (TTA), two triplets meet. One is lifted to the bright singlet S1, and the other drops to the ground state. In OLEDs this raises χ from 0.25 to 0.625, and most commercial blue OLEDs rely on it. Its potential to raise the voltage of solar cells had not been considered, although TTA had been reported in fullerene cells. (A related explainer covers the other factors.)

2 / 9 · TWO ACCEPTORS

Same donor, swapped core

Y6 and Y11 are both A–DA′D–A acceptors from the Y-series. They differ only in the central acceptor unit A′: Y6 carries benzothiadiazole (BT), and Y11 carries benzotriazole (BTz).

Blended with the donor polymer PM6, both give efficient cells with nearly the same numbers: 15.2% for PM6:Y6 and 15.1% for PM6:Y11. Their triplets, however, behave very differently.

3 / 9 · WATCHING TRIPLETS APPEAR

Triplets grow after the holes have left

Femtosecond transient absorption (TAS) follows the excited states. An 800 nm pulse excites mainly the acceptor. A photoinduced absorption (PIA) at 1500–1600 nm appears first. It belongs to an intermolecular charge-transfer state between neighbouring acceptor molecules (inter-CT), and it fades within about 100 ps as holes move to PM6.

After that, a second PIA grows at 1400–1450 nm: the acceptor triplet exciton, assigned from earlier sensitisation experiments on Y6. Its growth from about 100 ps onward depends on the excitation fluence. That makes it a bimolecular process, so the triplets come from free charges that meet again (non-geminate recombination).

4 / 9 · OPPOSITE TRENDS

In Y11, more light means faster triplet loss

The kinetics are normalised to the inter-CT signal at time zero. In PM6:Y6, a higher fluence makes the triplet maximum come earlier and sit higher, as expected: more charges, more recombination. PM6:Y11 does the opposite. At higher fluence its triplets start to decay at a lower normalised value.

Such rapid, strongly fluence-dependent quenching means the triplets are lost by a process of higher order in the triplet density than TCA. The authors fit the data with

dnT/dt = −α dnH/dt − β nHnT − γ nT2

Here α is the fraction of recombination that forms triplets, β is the TCA rate constant and γ is the TTA rate constant. Monomolecular decay is left out, because the triplets live longer than the 2 ns window.

5 / 9 · THE FIT

TCA in Y6, TTA in Y11

The cross-sections of the hole polaron and the triplet are hard to measure, so the fit uses a "cross-section free" form with parameters A, B and G proportional to α, β and γ. In PM6:Y6, B = (6.3 ± 0.6) × 1010 s−1 and G = (7 ± 2) × 1010 s−1 are similar. Polarons far outnumber triplets, though, so TCA dominates. In PM6:Y11 only G could be extracted, (2.3 ± 0.2) × 1011 s−1, and TCA is negligible. Because of the normalisation, these numbers do not compare the two blends quantitatively.

Neat films measured with nanosecond TAS give G = 1.46 × 1011 s−1 (Y11) and 1.20 × 1011 s−1 (Y6). The neat films are about half as thick, so the same γ should give twice the G. Y6 fits this rule. Y11 does not: the blend has a much faster TTA rate than the neat film. Unlike the blend, the neat Y11 film was not annealed. An unannealed PM6:Y11 blend gives G = (1.7 ± 0.3) × 1011 s−1, which points to better order speeding up TTA.

6 / 9 · MAGNETIC RESONANCE

Triplets that make light

Photoluminescence-detected magnetic resonance (PLDMR, at 10 K) flips triplet spin sublevels with microwaves. The PL changes only if the triplets are coupled to light emission, for example through TTA. The broad feature is the acceptor triplet (zero-field splitting D = 930 MHz in PM6:Y11 and 990 MHz in PM6:Y6, plus a half-field signal at 167 mT). The narrow centre peak at 336.1 mT comes from distant spins such as CT states.

The "wings" are steeper in PM6:Y11. Its ordering factor λθ = 9.0 against 5.5 in PM6:Y6 means more out-of-plane order. The triplet signal rises as power1.47 below about 10 mW and linearly above it, the pattern of TTA systems that become annihilation-limited. Relative to the CT peak, the triplet-sensitive PL is nine times larger in PM6:Y11 (0.45 against 0.05).

7 / 9 · WHY Y11 ORDERS

A bigger dipole, longer-range stacking

X-ray scattering (GIWAXS) shows that both neat acceptors stack face-on, with a π–π peak near q ≈ 1.7 Å−1. In annealed PM6:Y11, a new out-of-plane peak appears near q ≈ 0.6 Å−1, a sign of long-range order that PM6:Y6 lacks.

Density functional theory suggests why. BTz withdraws less electron density than BT, so in Y11 the end groups are no longer balanced by the core. Its dipole is 3.82 D, mostly in the molecular plane, against 0.97 D for Y6, mostly out of plane. A strong dipole pulls molecules together and drives crystallisation. More crystalline phases let triplets diffuse further, and TTA needs two triplets to meet.

8 / 9 · THE PRIZE

How many millivolts could fusion win back?

Suppose a fraction ω of the triplets made by recombination come back as singlets. ω can be at most 0.5, because every TTA event sends one triplet to the ground state. In the best case every recycled singlet emits (Eq. 6). In the other case it may split into charges again with probability ηCT = 0.93 and go round the cycle (Eq. 7):

χ = (1 − α) + αω · χ = [1 − α + αω(1 − ηCT)] / (1 − αωηCT)

Without TTA, χ = 1 − α. With α = 0.75 and ω = 0.5 the gain is about 24 mV in the best case and about 14 mV with re-dissociation. Near α = 0.95 it reaches about 60 and 28 mV. That is "several tens of mV" under the most favourable conditions.

9 / 9 · THE CATCH

Triplets must live about a microsecond

TTA is second order: it needs many triplets at once. Under 1 Sun the polaron density is about 4.5 × 1016 cm−3. If triplets live only 10–100 ns, they reach about 1015 cm−3 and simply decay on their own first. TTA becomes a major channel only if the triplet lifetime exceeds about 1 µs. The neat-film fits give 63 ± 7 ns for Y11 and 600 ± 400 ns for Y6. Some ITIC derivatives reach about 100 µs. The map assumes weak TCA, as in PM6:Y11. With the much stronger TCA of PM6:Y6, TTA stays minor (Fig. S20).

PM6:Y11 does lose less voltage (ΔVnr 220 mV against 260 mV for PM6:Y6), but probably not because of TTA. A smaller S1–CT offset or stronger hybridisation are likelier causes. The design rules: raise the intrinsic TTA rate through order (dipole, side chains, annealing, additives), and push the triplet lifetime toward about 1 µs. Longer-lived triplets also react more with oxygen, so the lifetime should not be pushed further than that.
