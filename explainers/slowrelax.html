Explainer · J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2021 · Paper

Charges that leave before they cool down

The usual theory of an organic solar cell's voltage assumes that light-generated charges have fully relaxed (thermalized) before anything else happens. This study tests that assumption by modelling one device in two ways. A drift–diffusion model builds in thermal equilibrium. A kinetic Monte Carlo model follows every hop of every charge. Only the Monte Carlo model gets the measured open-circuit voltage right. The equilibrium model comes out 0.1–0.2 V too low. Many charges reach the electrodes before they have relaxed in the disordered energy landscape, and that leftover energy shows up as extra voltage.

T. Upreti, S. Wilken, H. Zhang & M. Kemerink*. Slow relaxation of photogenerated charge carriers boosts open-circuit voltage of organic solar cells. J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 12, 9874–9881 (2021). doi:10.1021/acs.jpclett.1c02235 (* corresponding author)

1 / 9 · TWO KINDS OF RELAXATION

Charges slide slowly down a rough landscape

In an organic semiconductor, charges sit on molecules, and no two molecules have quite the same energy. The available energies form a broad, roughly Gaussian density of states (DOS) with width σ. A freshly made charge relaxes in two stages. First it couples to molecular vibrations and drops to the lowest excited state of its molecule. That takes less than a picosecond.

That molecule is usually not the lowest one around, though. To go lower, the charge has to hop from molecule to molecule. This is thermally activated tunnelling, and it is slow. If the charge waited long enough, it would settle about σ²/kT below the centre of the DOS. Earlier experiments and simulations showed that, in organic solar cells, charges are usually collected before this second stage is finished.

2 / 9 · THE EQUILIBRIUM RULEBOOK

Every standard loss term assumes cooled charges

The textbook expression for the open-circuit voltage is VOC = (kT/q)·ln(JSC/J0 + 1). JSC is the short-circuit current. J0 is the dark saturation current, which holds all recombination losses. A reciprocity relation (Rau) gives J0 from the photovoltaic quantum efficiency, the electroluminescence efficiency EQEEL and the blackbody spectrum.

Three loss channels are usually counted. The first is the driving force from the donor/acceptor energy offset, still 0.2–0.3 V. The second is non-radiative recombination, kT/q·ln(EQEEL), at least ~0.2 V even in the best cells. The third is energetic disorder: the wider the DOS, the deeper charges sink and the lower VOC. All three assume, openly or not, that the charges are in thermal equilibrium with the lattice. That is a safe assumption for silicon, where charges cool in under a picosecond. It is less safe here.

3 / 9 · TWO MODELS, ONE DEVICE

Follow every hop, or assume equilibrium

The authors use a kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) model they had calibrated against experiments. It follows each charge hopping through a Gaussian DOS, so slow relaxation is built in. It also includes injection from the contacts, charge-transfer (CT) recombination and a simple morphology: a mixed TQ1:PC71BM matrix with embedded PC71BM aggregates. KMC is the gold standard for charge transport in these materials.

Then they feed the same parameters into a drift–diffusion (DD) model. It uses the same injection barriers and the same gap between the donor HOMO and the acceptor LUMO. Disorder enters through established mobility functions. The one real difference is that DD uses Boltzmann statistics, so it assumes the charges have thermalized. Any mismatch between the two therefore measures the effect of non-equilibrium.

4 / 9 · THE MISSING 0.2 V

Equilibrium undershoots the voltage

For a 70 nm TQ1:PC71BM cell, the KMC model reproduces the measured J–V curve, open-circuit voltage included. The DD model gets the short-circuit current and the general shape about right, but its VOC is about 0.2 V too low: 0.66 V, against 0.88 V from KMC.

A third, independent estimate agrees with DD. Inserting the KMC input parameters into eq. 1 under strict equilibrium gives VOC ≈ 0.69 V. So if the device were in thermal equilibrium, it would lose about 0.2 V more than it actually does. In the authors' words, the device operates as a hot-carrier solar cell.

5 / 9 · NOT A THICKNESS ARTEFACT

The gap stays at every thickness

Changing the active-layer thickness changes how long charges need to get out and how dense they are, so the authors made cells from about 65 to 310 nm. They used transfer-matrix modelling to get the generation profile for each thickness. KMC tracks the measured VOC (0.88–0.90 V) throughout. DD stays near 0.68–0.71 V, and the gap does not close.

DD also makes VOC follow the interference ripple in the photocurrent, just as eq. 1 predicts. So does the analytical Blakesley–Neher model, which shrinks the effective gap by σ²/kT. Neither the experiment nor KMC shows that link. Why no clear drop for thicker films? Relaxation in disordered media goes roughly linearly in log(time), so the modest change in extraction time across this thickness range matters little. Both models describe JSC well, and the fill factor reasonably well.

6 / 9 · WHERE THE CHARGES GO

Even at open circuit, most charges leave

KMC can tell where each photogenerated charge ends up. The total escape yield counts every charge that exits through either contact. The net escape yield counts exits through the correct contact, minus exits through the wrong one. At short circuit the two differ a lot. Despite a built-in field of ~1 V, many charges leave through the wrong, non-selective contact. That points to fast, diffusive, non-equilibrium motion that only a large field can suppress.

At VOC, about 80% of the photogenerated charges do not recombine at all; they leave through one of the contacts. About 30% leave through the correct contact. CT recombination saturates already at small forward bias, which rules out field-dependent generation as the reason for the unsaturated photocurrent.

7 / 9 · TWO CURRENTS IN BALANCE

Hot charges out, cool charges in

At VOC the net current is zero. So if ~30% of the photogenerated charges are still extracted at the correct contact, an equal injection current must flow the other way. The contacts inject these charges, and the contacts act as thermal reservoirs, so the injected charges are thermalized.

That splits the device into two populations. The photogenerated charges are hot and recombine weakly; most of them leave. The injected charges are cool and recombine strongly. This fits an earlier observation that recombination near VOC in this system follows near-equilibrium rates. It also explains why equilibrium concepts have seemed to work so well.

8 / 9 · TEMPERATURE, AND A MODERN BLEND

Same story for PM6:Y6

The extra energy of the photocharges acts like a carrier temperature above the lattice temperature, so temperature-dependent J–V curves are a strong test. For a 75 nm TQ1:PC71BM cell, KMC follows the measured VOC from 150 to 295 K, with the same parameters as before. At room temperature DD sits 0.17 V below. The slope of VOC versus T is similar in both models, because it is set by the injection current, which is thermalized in both.

Is this only an issue for old, strongly disordered fullerene blends? The authors repeated the test on a 115 nm PM6:Y6 cell, a 16%-class non-fullerene blend. Its disorder (σHOMO ≈ 89 meV, σLUMO ≈ 68 meV) is well above kT ≈ 25 meV. The difference is smaller, 0.13 V at room temperature, but DD still undershoots and KMC still fits.

9 / 9 · CAN EQUILIBRIUM BE SAVED?

Reciprocity breaks when only one side is hot

In the dark, every charge is injected from a thermal contact, yet the KMC dark current still turns on at a higher voltage than in DD. In a Shockley picture that means a smaller J0. To get it from reciprocity, part of the absorption spectrum would have to give no current. Matching the KMC value of 0.88 V would need the internal quantum efficiency (IQE) to fall to zero below ~1.12 eV. The IQE calculated by KMC actually rolls off near 0.86 eV. Using that IQE gives only ~0.73 V. Reciprocity assumes that injection and extraction mirror each other. That fails when one channel is far from equilibrium and the other is not.

Why did earlier VOC predictions from measured EQEEL work? The authors suggest that errors cancel. The usual EQEEL of 10⁻⁵–10⁻⁶ implies a 0.3–0.35 V non-radiative loss. An EQEEL near 10⁻², set by the ratio of S1 and CT lifetimes, gives ~0.12 V. That difference is close to the ~0.2 V found here. A higher VOC does not automatically mean higher efficiency, because JSC and FF count too. Whether hot charges can be used to beat the Shockley–Queisser limit, or to make thicker cells, is left open.

Try it

How far does a charge relax before it leaves?

A toy, not a simulation from the paper. A charge starts at the centre of a Gaussian DOS of width σ and loses energy linearly in log(time). It reaches the equilibrium depth σ²/kT after a toy time teq = t0·10(σ/kT)², with t0 = 1 ps. Both constants are invented. They only mimic the strong growth of the relaxation time with σ/kT seen in Monte Carlo studies. Choose when the charge is extracted and read off how much energy it still carries above equilibrium. This energy excess is a toy number, not the paper's VOC gain.

Try this: at σ = 68 meV (PM6:Y6 LUMO, from the paper) and 295 K, the toy charge needs about 15 µs to reach equilibrium; extracted after 10 ns it still carries ~80 meV above it. Lower σ to ~35 meV and it relaxes within ~0.1 ns. Cooling the device makes σ/kT larger, so relaxation becomes even slower. In the paper the voltage gain is not simply this energy surplus, because cool injected charges set the recombination (step 7).
